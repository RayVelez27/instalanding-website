§ 02 — Premise

A model's summary of its own run is a story about the run, written by the thing being investigated. Spool records the primary sources instead: the calls as issued, the results as returned, signed at the boundary, with the clock and the token counter running the whole time. You get a transcript you can read out loud in a post-mortem.

Every step, not a sample

Reasoning, tool call, result, retry, write. Each one timestamped, measured and attributed to the model that produced it.

Overhead — 1.4% of wall clock

Results signed at the boundary

Tool results are signed where they enter the loop and verified on the way back in, so a result the model wrote itself cannot pass as one a tool returned.

Detects fabricated results

Playable, not just greppable

Scrub the tape, watch the context window fill, see the spend accumulate against its cap, and stop on the step where it went wrong.

1

Kind is one of five, and it is what the tape draws: solid for a tool call, hatched for reasoning, striped for a write, red for a retry or a halt. You can read the shape of a run without reading a word of it.

2

The call as issued, not the model's description of the call. Those two disagree more often than anyone would like.

3

The response the tool actually gave back, including the status. A 429 here is why six per cent of this run is spent waiting.

4

Latency for the step alone. Reasoning is slow and expensive in tokens; a rate-limited call is slow and costs almost none. On the tape they look nothing alike, which is the point.

5

Tokens in and out, billed. Times the rate, that is the step's share of the spend — the number the gauge is accumulating while it plays.

6

Context after the step. Watch it halve at step thirteen, where compaction ran and fourteen older tool results were summarised away.

7

What changed in the run's state. Four steps in a row that change nothing is the signature of a run that has stopped making progress.

8

The signature, verified where the result entered the loop. An unsigned result is one the model produced itself, and it is flagged rather than trusted.

        § 10 — Questions
      

      

        

          01 Does recording slow the agent down?
          
About 1.4% of wall clock, nearly all of it the signature at the tool boundary. The recorder never sits between the model and the network — it watches a copy and writes it out of band, so a slow collector costs you a tape, not a run.

        

        

          02 What happens to the tape afterwards?
          
Full tape for thirty days, headers and metadata for a year, and an export to Parquet whenever you want it. On the annual plan the collector runs inside your own network and nothing leaves it at all.

        

        

          03 Can it record a run that has already failed?
          
That is most of what it records. A halted run is the interesting one: the tape keeps everything up to the stop, including the step that caused it and the state the run was in when it happened.

        

        

          04 How big is a tape?
          
The run above is twenty-four steps and about forty kilobytes compressed. Tool results dominate, so a run that reads a lot of files is larger than one that thinks a lot; a four-hundred-step run is still under a megabyte.

        

        

          05 Is any of this real?
          
No. Spool is a fictional product built as a design demo, and the run, the models, the companies, the quotes, the timings and the prices were all invented for it. Nothing is recorded here and nothing leaves this page.

      ABOUT THE RECORDER
      
    

    

      Field Manual · Section 1
      

        
        

      

      
An agent run is a sequence of decisions, and by the time anyone looks at it the only surviving witness is the model that made them. Ask it what happened and you get a summary — fluent, plausible, and written by the party under investigation. The tape is the alternative: the calls as they were issued, the results as they were returned, in order, with the clock running.

      
think — what it decided to do
tool — what it asked for, and what came back
write — what it changed
retry — what it had to do twice

      
Each step carries its latency, its tokens in and out, the running spend against the run's cap, and the state of the working context. Reasoning steps are cheap in wall clock and expensive in tokens; tool calls are the reverse; retries are where both go to die. Reading a run is mostly looking for the moment those two lines diverge.

      
This tape ends in a halt. On its last step the run tried to send the report it had just written, and the send was outside the policy it was given — a terminal stop, filed under 5.7.0. The work it did before that is still good; it is sitting in the draft, waiting for a person to press send.

      
Scrub the tape with a click or a drag, step with the arrow keys, play with space, and change speed with 1, 2 and 3. Every number on this page is invented.
